## Bottom line

In this house, heat fails before food does. An oil boiler needs roughly 300–800 W of electricity for its burner, controls and circulator. Without power, a Rhode Island house in November loses heat within hours and risks frozen pipes within a few days.

So the plan is built around one goal: **keep the boiler running safely**. That means a generator inlet installed by an electrician, a dual-fuel inverter generator, and a rationed fuel plan. Water, food, light and communications come next.

The plan is layered: **72 hours → 2 weeks → 30 days → 90 days.** Each layer covers outages that are far more likely than the next. A true 90-day grid-down event is rare, so almost everything here also pays off in an ordinary Nor'easter outage.

One honest limit: 90 days of generator fuel can't be stored safely at home. The stored fuel covers about 6–9 weeks of rationed running. After that, the plan relies on refills or on falling back to one heated room. Cost estimates are approximate as of late 2026; check prices before buying.

## Do this week

If we only finish this list, we're ready for a 2-week winter outage. The items are in priority order.

- [ ] **Book a licensed electrician** to install a generator inlet and an interlock (or a manual transfer switch), with a permit. Also ask them about a plug-in transfer switch just for the boiler. Electricians book up after the first big storm, so call first.
- [ ] **Top off the oil tank** and ask the oil company how deliveries work during regional outages.
- [ ] **Ask the oil-burner technician** how to protect the heating loop if the boiler is down for days (draining it, or adding glycol). Write the steps down.
- [ ] **Buy a dual-fuel inverter generator** with 4,000–5,000 W running output, plus a heavy-duty cord rated for the inlet.
- [ ] **Battery-powered CO alarms**: one on every level and one outside each sleeping area.
- [ ] **Water, first 2 weeks**: about 60 gallons of bottled water, plus 2 bathtub water bladders.
- [ ] **Refill every prescription** and ask the pharmacy and insurer about 90-day supplies.
- [ ] **Cash**: $500–1,000 in small bills.
- [ ] **Hand-crank NOAA weather radio** with AM/FM, plus headlamps and LED lanterns.
- [ ] **Indoor-rated propane heater** (low-oxygen shutoff and tip-over switch), with 1-lb cylinders.
- [ ] **Two weeks of no-cook food** the family will actually eat.
- [ ] **Turn on alerts**: phone Wireless Emergency Alerts, plus Cumberland's town notification sign-up.
- [ ] **Keep both cars at least half full** of gas from now on.
- [ ] **Print this plan** and the contacts page, and keep them with the radio.

## Heat and power

A 4,000–5,000 W dual-fuel inverter generator, run in scheduled blocks, keeps the boiler, refrigerator and freezer going on about 6–9 weeks of stored fuel.

### Connect it the right way

- **Never backfeed the house** through a dryer outlet with a double-male cord. It can electrocute utility lineworkers and start fires.
- **Use an inlet plus an interlock**, installed by a licensed electrician with a permit. Expect roughly $600–1,500 installed. Before starting the generator, switch the main breaker off, then turn on only the circuits we need.
- **Pick the generator circuits** now and label them: boiler, refrigerator, freezer, a few lights and outlets, and the sump pump if we have one.
- **Leave off** the electric stove, any electric water heater, and the dryer. These draw more than the generator can spare.
- **Hot water**: many oil boilers heat household water through a coil, which works whenever the boiler runs. Confirm how ours works.

### Fuel plan (approximate)

| Fuel | Amount stored | Run time at moderate load | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gasoline | about 20 gal in 4 × 5-gal cans | about 65–80 hrs | Add fuel stabilizer. Store in a detached shed if possible. Ask Cumberland Fire about storage limits. |
| Propane, 100-lb cylinders | 3 cylinders (300 lb) | about 200 hrs | Delivered by a propane company. Store upright, outdoors. |
| Propane, 20-lb tanks | 4 tanks (80 lb) | about 50 hrs | The same tanks also fit a grill or camp stove. |
| **Total** | | **about 315–330 hrs** | About 52 days at 6 hrs/day, or about 80 days at 4 hrs/day |

Run time assumes roughly 0.25–0.3 gal of gasoline or 1.5 lb of propane per hour at 25–50% load. Check the generator's manual and log actual use during the first week.

**Run schedule:** for the first week, run three blocks a day of about 2 hours each (morning, late afternoon, bedtime). That heats the house, recovers hot water, and keeps the fridge and freezer cold. Cut back to two blocks once we know how long the outage will last. Cook, charge devices and do laundry during the blocks.

### Quiet power between blocks

A 1–2 kWh LiFePO4 power station with a 200–400 W folding solar panel covers phones, lights, radios, and a CPAP if anyone uses one. November sun in RI is weak, so the generator does most of the recharging. Ask the electrician whether the boiler-only transfer switch could also take a power station, to carry the boiler for a few hours overnight.

### If the generator fails: the winter room

- Choose one interior room on the main floor and close off the rest of the house.
- Hang blankets over the doorways and cover the windows with insulation film or bubble wrap.
- Everyone sleeps in that room in sleeping bags rated to 0–20°F, with wool socks and hats.
- Run the indoor-rated propane heater only while awake, with a window cracked about 1 inch and a CO alarm in the room. Never run it while sleeping.
- **Pipe trigger:** if the indoor temperature drops toward 40°F with no fix in sight, shut off the main water, open the faucets, drain the lines, and put RV antifreeze in the toilets and drains. Have the boiler loop drained or protected using the technician's steps.
- **Leave trigger:** if the house stays below about 45°F for more than a day, go to a warming center or to relatives. Call 2-1-1 to find one.

### Safety

- Keep the generator **at least 20 ft from the house**, with the exhaust pointed away from doors and windows. Never run it in the garage, even with the door open.
- Get a generator tent or cover so it can run in snow, and chain-lock it, since generators get stolen during outages.
- Keep an ABC fire extinguisher on each floor. Refuel only when the generator is cool.

## Water

Store at least 360 gallons of drinking water: 1 gallon per person per day, for 4 people, for 90 days. Add filtering and treatment for anything beyond that.

City water often keeps flowing for a while after the power goes out, because utilities use elevated tanks and backup generators. In a long outage, though, expect pressure drops and boil-water notices. Plan as if the tap could stop.

| Source | Capacity | Cost (approx.) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 × 55-gal food-grade drums | 330 gal | $400–550 with pump and wrench | Fill from the tap. City chlorine keeps it fresh. Each full drum weighs about 460 lb, so place them on the basement slab first. Replace the water every 12 months. |
| Bottled water | about 60 gal | $60–90 | Grab-and-go, and covers the first 2 weeks. |
| 2 bathtub bladders | about 200 gal | $60–80 | Fill these the moment an outage looks long. |
| Pots, pitchers and jugs | about 20 gal | — | Fill them in the first hour. |
| Rain barrel or a bucket under a downspout | varies | $100 | Use for toilet flushing and cleaning only. |

**Making water safe**

- **Filter**: a gravity filter with replacement elements, rated for bacteria and protozoa. About $150–350.
- **Bleach**: plain, unscented household bleach. Use 8 drops per gallon for 6% bleach, or 6 drops for 8.25%. Stir, then wait 30 minutes. Bleach weakens over time, so buy a fresh jug every year.
- **Boil**: a rolling boil for 1 minute, using butane or generator time.
- **Separate the supplies**: label drinking water and flushing water so nobody mixes them up.

**If the taps stop**, flush a toilet by pouring about 1–1.5 gallons of non-drinking water fast into the bowl. Only flush solids. For everything else, use the bucket toilet in the sanitation section.

## Food and cooking

Plan for about 10,000 calories a day, roughly 900,000 for 90 days. The two adults need around 2,000–2,400 a day each and the two late teens around 2,400–3,000. Buy what the family already eats, and rotate it into normal meals.

**Eating order:** refrigerator food on days 1–2, then freezer food (cooked during generator blocks), then canned food, then bulk staples.

**Cooking without the electric stove**

- **During generator blocks** (most efficient): a pressure cooker such as an Instant Pot (about 1,000 W, cooks rice and beans fast), an electric kettle, a slow cooker, and a 1,800 W induction hot plate. Run one heavy appliance at a time.
- **Indoors, backup**: a butane burner rated for indoor use, with a window cracked and the CO alarm nearby. Stock 30–40 canisters.
- **Outdoors**: the propane grill or a 2-burner camp stove, using the shared 20-lb tanks.
- **Supplies**: a manual can opener (get 2), a cast-iron pan, a big stockpot, paper plates and bowls to save dishwater, foil, zip bags, and a large cooler.

## Medical, hygiene and sanitation

The biggest medical risk in a long outage is running out of a prescription, so start with that.

**Medical**

- [ ] A 90-day supply of every prescription, plus paper copies of the prescriptions. Plan cold storage for any medicine that needs refrigeration.
- [ ] Over-the-counter basics: acetaminophen, ibuprofen, antihistamine, anti-diarrheal, oral rehydration salts, antacid, cough and cold medicine, hydrocortisone, and antibiotic ointment.
- [ ] An expanded first-aid kit: a tourniquet, pressure bandages, burn gel and dressings, a SAM splint, nitrile gloves, eye wash, 2 thermometers, and a pulse oximeter.
- [ ] Spare glasses, extra contacts and solution, and a temporary dental filling kit.
- [ ] A Red Cross first aid and CPR class for all four of us.

**Hygiene (90 days, 4 people)**

- [ ] Toilet paper: about 120 rolls. Also paper towels, baby wipes for bathing, hand sanitizer, and dry shampoo.
- [ ] Soap, toothpaste, deodorant, razors, and 90 days of period products if anyone needs them.
- [ ] Laundry: a plunger-style hand washer, 2 buckets, an indoor drying line, and detergent.

**Sanitation (if the water stops)**

- [ ] Two 5-gal bucket toilets with snap-on seats, heavy contractor bags, and 40 lb of clumping cat litter or sawdust.
- [ ] Disinfectant, bleach, and dish gloves.
- [ ] Trash: pickup may stop for weeks. Keep heavy bags, lidded outdoor bins, and a bungee strap to keep animals out.

## Communications, information and light

Without internet, the radio is our news source and text messages are the best way to reach people.

**Communications**

- [ ] Hand-crank or solar NOAA weather radio with AM/FM, plus a second battery-powered AM/FM radio. Major regional AM news stations include WPRO 630 (Providence) and WBZ 1030 (Boston).
- [ ] Cell towers run on backup batteries that may last hours or days. Text instead of calling, keep phones in low-power mode, and give each person a USB power bank.
- [ ] 4 GMRS handheld radios for family and neighborhood range. One FCC license, about $35, covers the whole family with no exam. License-free FRS radios are an alternative.
- [ ] Optional: amateur (ham) radio. The entry-level Technician license is a weekend of study and connects you to RI's emergency radio volunteers.
- [ ] A written family plan: a meeting point, an out-of-state contact, and phone numbers on paper in every wallet.

**Information offline**

- [ ] A printed copy of this plan, a paper RI road atlas, and a first-aid manual.
- [ ] Downloaded offline maps, an offline Wikipedia (Kiwix), ebooks, movies, music, and any school material, saved to a laptop and tablets.

**Lighting**

- [ ] 4 LED lanterns, 1 headlamp per person plus 2 spares, and stick-up tap lights for the stairs and bathroom.
- [ ] Rechargeable AA and AAA batteries with a USB charger, plus about 48 AA and 24 AAA alkalines for backup.
- [ ] Solar garden stake lights: charge them outside by day and bring them in at night.
- [ ] Avoid candles. If we do use them, keep them in jars and never leave them unattended.

## Documents, cash, security, tools and morale

Over 90 days, morale and roles matter as much as supplies. Give each teen a real job.

**Documents and cash**

- [ ] Keep $1,000+ in small bills, since card readers and ATMs go down with the power.
- [ ] A waterproof folder with copies of IDs, insurance policies, the deed, prescriptions, and photos of the home's contents. Keep a backup on an encrypted USB drive.
- [ ] One go-bag per person, in case the leave trigger in the heat section is reached.

**Security**

- [ ] Use blackout curtains at night. A lit house on a dark street advertises a generator and supplies.
- [ ] Know the neighbors, and agree on check-ins and sharing (tools, a chainsaw, generator time for anyone on medical equipment).
- [ ] Chain-lock the generator and fuel, and keep the supplies out of sight.

**Winter tools**

- [ ] Snow shovels, ice melt, a roof rake, a bow saw or chainsaw for limbs, work gloves, tarps, plastic sheeting, duct tape, rope, and a multi-tool.

**Morale and roles**

- [ ] Board games, card games, puzzles, books, journals, instruments, and offline media for each person.
- [ ] Assign roles, such as a water and supply officer, a generator and fuel log keeper, a cooking rotation, and a radio and news briefer.
- [ ] Keep a daily routine: fixed wake-up and meal times, schoolwork, exercise, and one shared activity each evening.
- [ ] If we have pets, add 90 days of their food, water, and medicine.

## When the power goes out: the first 72 hours

1. **Hour 0:** Report the outage to Rhode Island Energy: text OUTAGE to 743674, or call 1-855-743-1102. Note the time.
2. **Hour 0–1:** Turn on the AM radio to learn how big the outage is. Fill the bathtub bladders and every pot while the water pressure holds. Unplug sensitive electronics.
3. **Hours 1–6:** Keep the fridge and freezer closed. If it's cold out, or the outage looks like it will last more than a few hours, set up the generator outside. Then switch the main breaker off, engage the interlock, start the generator, and turn on the boiler circuit.
4. **Day 1:** Start the generator block schedule and the fuel log. Set up the winter room as a backup. Assign roles. Check on neighbors.
5. **Days 2–3:** Eat the refrigerator food. Cook freezer meat during generator blocks. Check that the stored water is untouched and the CO alarms work.
6. **Week 1 onward:** Compare actual fuel use to the plan, and cut to 2 blocks a day once the outage is clearly long. Reorder propane while suppliers are still delivering.

## Budget and upkeep

The full 90-day kit costs roughly $7,500–12,900. A true 90 days of heat, water and food for four people costs a lot more than 30 days does. The line items are in the [tracker spreadsheet](90-Day-Prep-Tracker.xlsx).

| Phase | When | Core items ($) | Optional items ($) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Safety and the first 2 weeks | This week | 1,240–1,980 | — |
| 2. Generator, fuel and water storage | Within 2–4 weeks | 3,800–6,680 | 940–1,720 |
| 3. Food, medical and supplies to 90 days | Within 60 days | 2,440–4,260 | 480–800 |
| **Total** | | **7,490–12,930** | **1,420–2,520** |

Not included: the heating oil top-off, prescription copays, and the cash reserve, which is set aside rather than spent.

**Ways to land closer to $6,000**

- **Do phases 1 and 2 fully, then build food over time.** Count what's already in the pantry, and add a few extra items on every grocery trip. Phases 1 and 2 cover the most likely outage, a winter storm lasting 1–3 weeks.
- **Buy at the low end on the big items**: a generator around $1,000, filled 20-lb tanks instead of 100-lb cylinders, and a basic water filter. Using only 20-lb tanks cuts propane run time by roughly 140 hours.
- **Skip the optional items** (power station, solar panel, go-bags, rain barrel, class) until later.
- **Don't cut** the inlet installation, the CO alarms, or the prescriptions.

**Upkeep**

- [ ] Monthly: run the generator for 20 minutes under load, test the CO alarms, and eat and replace a few food items.
- [ ] Every 6 months, at the clock changes: check batteries, lanterns and radios, and recharge the power station and power banks.
- [ ] Every October: put the stored gasoline in the cars and refill the cans, replace the drum water, buy fresh bleach, check medicine expiration dates, and get the boiler serviced and the oil tank topped off.
